# PART 1 — THE NUMBERS

## 1.1 Why automotive agency rates don't transfer

The obvious benchmark is automotive, since that's where agency is established. <cite index="62-1">In the automotive agency model the dealer facilitates the transaction and receives a commission, typically 5–8% of the vehicle price, with the manufacturer controlling pricing and owning the customer relationship</cite>. European implementations run <cite index="66-1">somewhere between 6% and 10% on the front-end margin</cite>.

**Do not apply 5–8% to a bicycle.** Car dealers historically ran front-end margins around 8–10%, so agency at 5–8% is a modest trim. Bike shops run 30–34%. The percentage is doing completely different work in the two industries.

There's also a warning in that data. <cite index="63-1">In the NFDA Dealer Attitude Survey, Mercedes dealers scored total margin on new cars at 4.9, down sharply from 8.9 in the previous survey</cite> — and <cite index="71-1">JLR, Lotus and Ford have all experimented with agency and since reverted</cite>. Agency fails when the commission doesn't compensate for the effort. **Err generous at the founding tier.** You're recruiting on faith, and a partner who feels short-changed in year one won't be there in year three.

## 1.2 The correct method: decompose the traditional margin

A conventional ~32% bike margin is paying for five different things. Under agency, you absorb two of them.

| What the 32% covers | Approx. | Under agency |
|---|---|---|
| Stock financing and carrying cost | 4–6% | **Alkota absorbs** |
| Markdown / model-year obsolescence risk | 5–8% | **Alkota absorbs** |
| Selling effort and customer acquisition | 8–10% | Partner |
| Assembly, PDI, fit, handover labour | 5–7% | Partner — **paid separately** |
| Overhead contribution and profit | 6–8% | Partner |

Removing the two risk components leaves roughly **18–24%** of economic value with the partner, plus the labour paid as a discrete fee rather than buried in margin.

Paying labour separately matters more than it looks. It makes the workshop time visible and defensible, it stops the fit session being treated as a free pre-sale cost, and it means the partner's earnings don't collapse if you ever adjust RRP.

## 1.3 Recommended commission

| | **Foundation Partner** | **Certified Partner** |
|---|---|---|
| Complete bike commission | **20%** of RRP ex-VAT | **17%** of RRP ex-VAT |
| Fit, build & handover fee | **£350** per bike | **£350** per bike |
| First service (at ~6 weeks) | **£85**, reimbursed | Partner's own pricing |
| P&A / apparel | 50% of SRP | 50% of SRP |
| Warranty labour | £65/hr reimbursed | £65/hr reimbursed |
| Service revenue thereafter | 100% partner | 100% partner |

**What that's worth per bike.** You haven't set RRP, so as a sensitivity: at a £7,000 ex-VAT RRP, a Foundation Partner earns £1,400 commission + £350 build + £85 first service = **£1,835 per bike, with zero stock risk and zero capital deployed.** At £9,000 it's £2,235.

Compare to a conventional 32% margin on the same bike: £2,240 — but the shop has funded the unit, carried it, and risked marking it down. **Risk-adjusted, 20% plus fees is competitive with 32% distribution**, and it's a much easier conversation with a shop that's cash-constrained.

The 3-point gap between tiers is deliberate: enough that Foundation status is visibly worth having and worth keeping, not so much that Certified Partners feel like second-class citizens.

## 1.4 The fit/build/handover fee

Grounded in real labour cost. <cite index="60-1">The UK independent garage labour-rate average sits around £79–81/hour ex-VAT in mid-2026, up over 60% since 2020</cite>. Specialist bike workshops sit below automotive but a good MTB shop is not cheap — <cite index="56-1">a full service at an independent London workshop is £220 labour-only, with a master service at £350</cite>.

Realistic time on a high-end made-to-order bike:

| Task | Hours |
|---|---|
| Fit consultation and size confirmation | 1.0–1.5 |
| Unbox, build, torque protocol, documentation | 1.5–2.0 |
| Suspension setup to rider weight and preference | 0.75–1.0 |
| Handover, walkthrough, registration | 0.75–1.0 |
| **Total** | **4.0–5.5** |

At £65–75/hr that's £260–£410. **£350 sits mid-range and is defensible in both directions** — generous enough that a shop doesn't resent the time, disciplined enough that it doesn't distort your unit economics.

Set the **warranty labour reimbursement at £65/hr**. Slightly below a shop's retail rate, which is normal and accepted, but high enough that warranty work isn't a punishment.

**Include a paid first service.** £85 at around six weeks, reimbursed by you. It costs little, it guarantees every bike gets checked after bed-in, it puts the rider back in the partner's workshop where the long-term relationship forms, and it's a genuine quality-control channel — your partners will tell you what's coming loose before a customer does.

## 1.5 P&A opening order — solving your cash-flow problem

You want commitment without scaring shops off, and without ruining your own cash flow. The answer is **staging, not sizing**.

**Recommended: no payment at signing. £1,200 ex-VAT opening order, payable on first bike delivery.**

Why this works both ways:

- **The shop** commits to a real number but pays nothing for two years. In a market where <cite index="43-1">revenue is declining at 3.2% annually</cite>, "sign now, pay at launch" is the difference between a yes and a maybe.
- **You** get a committed, contractual order book that lands exactly when you need working capital most — at production launch, when tooling and first-build costs have already been absorbed.
- It's a **qualifying filter**. A shop unwilling to commit £1,200 payable in 2028 was never going to sell this bike.

Structure it as: pivot bearings and hardware kits, torque-critical fasteners, consumables, and a small apparel allocation. Weight it toward **service parts, not merchandise** — a partner who holds your bearings can fix your bikes, and that's what protects the brand.

Add an escalating rebate rather than a bigger opening order if you want to encourage volume later: 2% additional commission above a stated annual unit count, reviewed yearly. Rewards performance without demanding capital up front.

## 1.6 Catchment radius

Radius confirmed. Recommended definition:

- **Foundation Partner: 40-mile radius** from the shop's registered trading address
- **Certified Partner: 25-mile radius**
- Where two catchments overlap, the lead routes to the **nearer** partner by road distance, not straight line
- Urban exception: in any area where population density would put more than [X] partners in one circle, catchment defaults to drive-time (45 minutes) rather than distance

40 miles is generous enough to be meaningful in the UK — it covers a real trading area for a destination MTB shop without carving the country into so few pieces that you can only ever appoint a dozen partners. Store the radius per-partner in the database so it's adjustable per agreement rather than hardcoded.

**Keep the language "first refusal within catchment", never "exclusive territory".** You retain the right to appoint a second partner if criteria lapse, and you avoid granting a perpetual veto over your own network.

# PART 3 — WHAT'S STILL OPEN

**You need to confirm before the terms move from DRAFT to APPROVED:**

1. The four figures above — 20% / 17% / £350 / £1,200. I'd treat 20% as the floor for Foundation, not a starting point for negotiation downward.
2. Foundation Partner cap — how many, and does it close when the founding register closes? Symmetry between the two would be neat.
3. Whether the 2% volume rebate goes in from day one or is held back.
4. Solicitor review of the agency structure. This is the one that actually gates everything — get the agency drafting wrong and you have an unlawful price-fixing arrangement rather than a lawful uniform price.

**A note on RRP.** Everything in Part 1 is expressed as a percentage precisely because you don't have a price yet. The moment you set one, the calculator makes these numbers concrete for a shop owner in about four seconds — which is exactly when the partner conversation gets real. Worth prioritising the landed-cost work.
