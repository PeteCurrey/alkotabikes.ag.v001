# PART 0 — THE MARKET YOU'RE RECRUITING INTO

Two facts should shape everything below.

**Shops are under real pressure.** UK bicycle retailing revenue is <cite index="43-1">expected to decline at a compound annual rate of 3.2% over the five years through 2025-26, to £1.6 billion, as the post-pandemic boom fades and rising living costs curb consumer spending</cite>. You are asking busy, squeezed retailers to invest attention in a brand that has no product for two years.

**Bike margin alone doesn't sustain a shop.** Complete-bike margins run <cite index="36-1">typically between 25% and 40% depending on brand and dealer level</cite>, with 30–34% the common average and 40% at the top end. But <cite index="37-1">the average break-even point for a specialty shop is around 37%, and manufacturer margin can vary by over 10% across dealer levels — the difference between 28% and 38% being "life and death" for a shop</cite>. Accessories carry far more: <cite index="40-1">retailers typically pay around 50% of SRP on parts and accessories</cite>. Shops survive on service and P&A, not on bikes.

**What that means for you:** competing on headline margin percentage is a losing game — you can't outbid Trek's volume rebates. Compete instead on *earnings per hour of shop attention*: high ticket value, near-zero stock risk, no discounting, and paid work attached to every unit.

# PART 1 — THE STRUCTURAL DECISION (read this first)

Before margin bands, you need to choose the **relationship model**, because it determines whether you can legally control retail price. This is the highest-leverage decision in the whole pack.

## Option A — Distribution (traditional)

Dealer buys at wholesale, owns the stock, resells at their own price.

**The problem:** under UK competition law you cannot set that price. <cite index="49-1">Imposing a fixed or minimum resale price is a hardcore restriction known as Resale Price Maintenance — one of the most serious competition law infringements and easy for authorities to establish. You may publish recommended retail prices or impose maximum prices, provided these do not in practice amount to fixed prices.</cite>

So a dealer can discount Project 01 the week after launch, and legally you can do nothing. For a boutique brand whose entire proposition is scarcity and integrity, discounting is the fastest route to brand death.

## Option B — Agency (recommended)

Alkota owns the bike until it reaches the customer. The partner introduces, fits, builds, hands over and services — and is paid a commission for doing so.

**Why this is the right structure for Alkota:**

- **You control the price legally.** <cite index="51-1">The Chapter I prohibition does not apply to undertakings forming part of a "single economic unit" — in competition law terms, the relationship between an agent and its principal is one in which the agent no longer acts as an independent economic operator.</cite> Genuine agency sits outside the RPM prohibition entirely. One price, everywhere, forever. No discounting, no grey market, no dealer undercutting your own site.
- **Zero stock risk for the partner.** In a declining market, this is the single most persuasive thing you can offer. You're asking for attention, not capital.
- **It matches the product.** Project 01 is made-to-order against a fit model. There is no inventory to hold.
- **It resolves the direct-sales conflict** (see §1.3).

**The catch — and it's a real one.** Genuine agency requires *you* to bear the commercial and financial risks: you own the stock, you carry transaction and credit risk, you bear the cost of unsold units. If the partner bears those risks, it isn't agency, it's distribution with a label on it, and the RPM protection evaporates. **This must be structured by a competition-aware solicitor.** Get it wrong and you have an unlawful price-fixing agreement rather than a clever one.

## Option C — Hybrid

Agency for complete bikes, distribution for P&A and merchandise (where margin is conventional and price control matters less).

**My recommendation: Option C.** Agency on Project 01 to protect price and remove partner risk; conventional wholesale on parts, apparel and consumables where the shop wants normal margin and you want none of the admin.

## 1.3 — The direct-sales question (answer this before recruiting)

You sell direct via `/order`. Every shop owner will ask, in the first five minutes: *"Do you undercut me?"* If you don't have an answer ready, the conversation ends.

**The recommended answer, which only works under agency:**

> One price everywhere. If a rider in your catchment buys — through you, through our site, or by walking in off the street — the fit, build, handover and first service happen at your workshop, and you're paid the full partner commission. We don't compete with you on price because there is only one price, and we don't compete with you for the customer because the customer ends up in your workshop either way.

That single term is worth more than five percentage points of margin. It converts your direct channel from a threat into a lead source for the partner, and it's the reason a good shop would take on an unproven brand.

# PART 2 — COMMERCIAL TERMS

Everything below is a **recommended starting position**, not a decided number. You need your landed cost and target RRP before any of it is real. The structure is what matters; the percentages are for you to set.

## 2.1 Partner commission / margin band

Two tiers, both earned on capability rather than volume.

| | **Foundation Partner** | **Certified Partner** |
|---|---|---|
| Cohort | Launch cohort, capped | Ongoing, from 2028 |
| Complete bike commission | Upper band | Standard band |
| P&A wholesale | 50% of SRP | 50% of SRP |
| Apparel / merch | 50% of SRP | 50% of SRP |
| Demo fleet | Alkota-supplied (§2.3) | Purchased at cost |
| Fit & build fee | Retained in full | Retained in full |
| Territory | First-refusal catchment | Non-exclusive |
| Listed on locator | Priority | Standard |
| Warranty labour | Reimbursed at agreed rate | Reimbursed at agreed rate |

**Setting the band.** Benchmark reality: 30–34% is the typical complete-bike margin, 28% is punishing, 38–40% is top-tier. Your advantages are that the partner carries no stock and takes no markdown risk, and that the ticket is high — so a lower percentage still produces a strong cash figure per unit. Your disadvantage is that you are unproven.

I'd set Foundation Partner commission at or slightly above the market midpoint and Certified below it, so that early risk is visibly rewarded and later entrants have a reason to want the earlier tier. **Do not go below the high twenties.** Below that you are asking a shop to lose money on attention, and the good ones can do that arithmetic in their heads.

**Three components, stated separately** — this matters, because it shows the partner where the money actually is:

1. **Sales commission** — % of retail, paid on every unit in catchment regardless of channel
2. **Fit, build and handover fee** — fixed £ per bike, retained in full, reflecting real workshop hours
3. **Service revenue** — 100% theirs, forever, and the reason the relationship compounds

## 2.2 Territory model

**Recommended: selective distribution with catchment first-refusal — not exclusivity.**

VABEO permits a good deal of flexibility here. <cite index="47-1">Selective and exclusive networks can be combined in the same or different geographical areas, and suppliers may appoint more than one exclusive distributor to the same territory ("shared exclusivity")</cite>. <cite index="51-1">Within a selective system you may restrict active and passive sales to unauthorised distributors.</cite>

**Why not hard exclusivity:** it's the thing dealers ask for and the thing that hurts you most. An exclusive partner who underperforms locks a region for years and is painful to remove. Exclusivity is also a promise you'd have to keep at the exact moment you most want to add coverage.

**What to offer instead:**
- A defined **catchment** (postcode districts or a stated radius), not a territory
- **First refusal** on any lead originating in that catchment
- A commitment not to appoint a second partner within the catchment while stated performance and capability criteria are met
- Explicit right to review annually against objective criteria

That gives a shop meaningful protection without granting a perpetual veto over your own network.

**Criteria must be objective, qualitative and applied uniformly** to stay inside the block exemption. Your existing APN-01 to APN-04 framework is well built for this because it's capability-based. Document *why* each criterion is necessary for correct product support — the "brand alignment" one is the softest and needs the clearest justification.

## 2.3 Demo terms — the crux

Nobody has ridden this bike. No review exists. No rider will spend Project 01 money on faith alone, and no shop will sell it without having ridden it.

**Recommendation: Alkota owns the demo fleet. Foundation Partners never buy a demo bike.**

Asking a shop to purchase a demo of an unproven brand, in a declining market, before a single review exists, is the number one reason a good shop says no. It's also unnecessary — the demo fleet is a marketing cost, not a revenue line, and treating it as one changes the entire conversation.

Structure:
- Alkota-owned, placed on loan, insured by Alkota, with a written loan agreement
- Partner commits to keeping it available, maintained and clean, and to running a stated minimum number of demo rides per season
- Demo-to-order conversion tracked — this is your single best partner performance metric, far better than units
- Rotation on a fixed schedule so no bike ages out in one shop
- Demo bikes retired and sold as ex-demo at a defined discount, with the partner taking commission on that sale
- **Certified Partners** (2028 onward) purchase demo units at cost with a buy-back or ex-demo commission arrangement

Add a **demo event programme**: Alkota-run days in the Peak District and at partner-nominated trail centres, with the partner attending. That solves the "nobody has ridden it" problem at the network level rather than shop by shop, and it feeds the content programme.

## 2.4 Stocking expectation

**Recommendation: no stock obligation on complete bikes.**

Project 01 is made to order against a fit model. There is nothing to stock, and a stocking requirement would be a fictional demand that serves only to look conventional.

What you *do* require:
- One demo bike available (Alkota-supplied for Foundation Partners)
- A **display commitment** — the bike properly presented, not wedged between two e-bikes. Specify what "properly presented" means and supply the fixtures.
- A P&A opening order — this is where a genuine stocking requirement belongs. Set it low enough to be reasonable and high enough to signal commitment.
- Consumables held for servicing: bearings, pivot hardware, torque-critical fasteners

**Set the P&A opening order in pounds, not units,** and make it the only financial commitment at signing. A partner should be able to join for a number they can approve without a board meeting.

## 2.5 Response SLAs — commit publicly

Santa Cruz's reputation runs substantially on service speed. A rider describes a claim as: <cite index="17-1">fill out an online form, attach a receipt, write a short description, and within a day an email from a friendly warranty rep and a replacement part on the way — "good warranty service can turn a frustrating situation into a brand-loyalty win"</cite>.

You cannot out-spend the incumbents. You can out-respond them, starting now, at zero cost.

| Commitment | Target |
|---|---|
| Partner application acknowledged | 2 working days |
| Application decision (including "not yet") | 10 working days |
| Trade enquiry response | 1 working day |
| Warranty claim first response | **2 working days** |
| Warranty part dispatched once approved | 5 working days |
| Technical support (workshop, bike on stand) | Same working day |
| Crash replacement quote | 3 working days |
| Order acknowledgement to customer | 1 working day |

**Publish these.** An SLA you publish is a promise; an SLA you keep privately is an aspiration. And answering "not yet" within ten working days is worth more to a busy shop owner than silence followed by a yes.

## 2.6 What each side commits to

**Partner commits to:**
- Completing Alkota technical certification before first delivery (build, torque protocol, suspension setup, warranty process)
- At least one certified technician on site, recertified annually
- Fit consultation using the Alkota fit model before any order
- Demo bike availability and a minimum number of demo rides per season
- Presenting the brand to the agreed standard
- Not listing Alkota product on third-party marketplaces — <cite index="45-1">VABEO guidance confirms that businesses can restrict the use of a specific sales channel, such as an online marketplace, regardless of distribution model, and still benefit from the block exemption</cite>
- Warranty administration to the published SLA
- Not exporting outside the network

**Alkota commits to:**
- One price everywhere, including our own site
- Full commission on every unit in catchment regardless of channel
- Demo fleet supplied and insured for Foundation Partners
- Technical training, free, in person before launch
- Warranty labour reimbursement at an agreed hourly rate
- Published SLAs, held
- Named contact — a person, not a portal
- Marketing assets, imagery and copy at no cost
- Named listing on the dealer locator
- No appointment of a second partner in catchment while criteria are met
- Advance notice of product and pricing changes before public announcement

# PART 3 — LEGAL GUARDRAILS

Take these to a competition-aware solicitor before anything becomes contractual. The safe harbour applies where <cite index="48-1">both parties hold less than 30% market share and the agreement contains no hardcore restrictions</cite> — you're comfortably inside on market share, so the entire risk is in the drafting.

**You must not:**
- Set a fixed or minimum resale price under a *distribution* model — RPM is hardcore and <cite index="49-1">easy for authorities to establish</cite>. Only a genuine agency relationship avoids this.
- Impose **wide retail parity obligations**. <cite index="50-1">VABEO treats wide retail parity obligations as hardcore restrictions, meaning agreements containing them can no longer benefit from the block exemption.</cite>
- Restrict passive sales — a partner must be able to respond to unsolicited enquiries from outside their catchment.
- Prevent effective use of the internet as a sales channel.
- Apply your selection criteria inconsistently between applicants.

**You may:**
- Publish an RRP, and impose *maximum* prices — <cite index="49-1">provided these do not in practice amount to fixed prices</cite>
- Operate selective distribution on objective qualitative criteria
- <cite index="51-1">Restrict active and passive sales to unauthorised distributors</cite>
- Ban specific channels such as online marketplaces
- Apply <cite index="45-1">different criteria for online and offline sales within a selective system, and charge a different wholesale price for online versus in-store sales (dual pricing)</cite> — though dual pricing must reflect genuine cost differences and must not be designed to prevent effective internet use
- Combine selective and exclusive models across different areas, and use shared exclusivity

**Note for Phase 7:** the US network needs separate treatment. US antitrust takes a materially different view — RPM is assessed under the rule of reason federally since *Leegin*, but several states are stricter, and the agency analysis differs. Don't assume the UK structure ports.

## THE ONE THING

If you take a single term from this pack, take **one price everywhere, full commission in catchment regardless of channel.** It's the term that resolves the direct-sales conflict, it's the reason a good shop takes on an unproven brand, and — structured as genuine agency — it's the only lawful way to stop your bike being discounted the month after launch.

It's also, conveniently, entirely consistent with the brand: no theatre, no clearance, no games.
